Data, method and limits

ARCNAVE lets anyone flip through real NASA SPHEREx images of the same patch of sky, taken months apart, and see what moved. This page says exactly where the data comes from, what the software does to it, how we tested it, and what it cannot do.

01What ARCNAVE is, and is not

In 1930 Clyde Tombaugh found Pluto by flipping between two photographs of the same stars, taken days apart: the stars stayed still, Pluto jumped. ARCNAVE does the same with SPHEREx, a NASA space telescope that photographs the whole sky every six months in 102 colours of near-infrared light. You pick any spot, the app fetches the real images of it, lines them up on the stars and shows you what changed.

No discovery is claimed. Every object ARCNAVE names is a known one, checked against an orbit catalogue. A change it cannot explain is labelled unconfirmed. Planet X (a hypothesised planet far beyond Neptune) is not confirmed to exist, and ARCNAVE does not search for it: the Hide Planet X tool only tests how faint a planet the method could catch.

02The data

Everything is fetched live from public archives (and kept in a cache on the server, so a spot opens in seconds the second time). Nothing is simulated except the clearly labelled fake planet in Hide Planet X.

About SPHEREx: launched 11 March 2025, it surveys the entire sky every six months in 102 colour bands from 0.75 to 5 microns (NASA JPL mission page). Each image covers one narrow slice of wavelength, so the colours you see in ARCNAVE are false colour.

New data releases. ARCNAVE asks IRSA's archive for every spot when you open it (the list is kept for one day), so images from a new SPHEREx release show up without any change to the code. The Agents panel names the release (for example QR2, Quick Release 2, from the file path) and the date of the newest image at that spot.

Contamination flags. Every SPHEREx image comes with a FLAGS plane from the pipeline. ARCNAVE leaves out pixels marked saturated, bleeding, halo or ghost, repairs single bad pixels (cosmic-ray hits, hot pixels) from their neighbours, and keeps "outlier"-only pixels for finding movers. Press Flags (Time machine or Blink) to see them on the image, with the counts for that image.

Light curves and deep views. Light curve follows one star's brightness at one colour through every pass, with steady comparison stars measured the same way; stars too bright for SPHEREx are measured from their outer glow (tested: about 0.1 mag scatter). Deep view adds up the images of each of SPHEREx's six detectors in one pass, so fainter things show; the noise drop is measured (1.7 to 2.6 times for 6 images on Barnard's Star's field). While building these we found that R Draconis and RW Aurigae are saturated at the centre in almost every image, so their earlier numbers were withdrawn and re-measured; the cards say so. On 30 Oct 2026 both were measured again at several colours: RW Aurigae faded by 0.74 mag from Sep 2025 to Sep 2026 in all six colours checked (3.9 to 4.8 µm, 49 images, about ten times the uncertainty), and R Draconis showed the same pulse at 0.75, 0.80 and 0.85 µm (25 images); steady comparison stars scatter by about 0.1 mag.

Checked against Astropy. The tool runs in your browser (JavaScript), so Astropy is used to check it: on two real SPHEREx files our sky-to-pixel maths, distortion included, agrees with astropy.wcs to 0.000″, and our predicted positions of Barnard's Star, Epsilon Indi B and WISE 0855 agree with Astropy's apply_space_motion within 0.07″ (tools/astropy_check.py).

Gaia stars and their motion. Press Gaia to see the stars measured by ESA's Gaia satellite (from NASA/IPAC IRSA), placed where they are on the date of the SPHEREx images, with arrows for the fast movers (20 years of motion). Tap one to see how far away it is and how fast it moves; for the fastest, Measure its motion checks it in the SPHEREx images, with slow stars as a comparison. Tested on Wolf 359 (4.1″ measured, 5.0″ predicted) and Kapteyn's Star (9.1″ measured, 8.6″ predicted).

Spectra on the fly. Press Spectrum and tap a star. SPHEREx's filters change colour across each detector, so in every image of a six-month pass the star is seen in a different colour: about 120 images give its brightness in about 100 colours from 0.75 to 5 µm. ARCNAVE reads only the few pixels around the star from NASA's archive (about 40 to 55 s for a pass), measures them with simple aperture photometry, leaves out colours where a flagged pixel sits on the star (and says why), and draws the 2MASS and WISE brightness of the same star as a check. Tested: a normal star agreed with 2MASS and WISE within 13 %; the brown dwarf Epsilon Indi B shows the water and methane dips expected for its type. This is a quick look, not the official SPHEREx spectra; very bright stars are saturated.

Embed it. Press Share, then Copy embed code: an <iframe> that shows only that patch of sky flipping through its SPHEREx images, with a link back to the full tool. Any link works too: ?embed=1#ra=269.45&dec=4.77&load=1, or ?embed=1#ct=barnard for a NASA test target.

Check it, then tell someone. In Changes, every spot that is unexplained or got brighter has links to the same position in SIMBAD, the Legacy Surveys viewer and the Minor Planet Center's asteroid checker. If it is still unexplained, Send this candidate opens your own e-mail app with a filled-in report (position, image, date, colour, signal, the checks already done and a link back to the view); you choose who gets it, ARCNAVE sends nothing itself. Copy report puts the same text on the clipboard, for example for the volunteers' Talk board of Backyard Worlds. The report always says "unconfirmed": most such spots are artefacts.

Take it to Python. Share → Python notebook downloads a Jupyter notebook for the spot you are looking at (Astropy + astroquery, in the style of IRSA's SPHEREx tutorials). It redoes ARCNAVE's work in the open: ARCNAVE's own query to NASA/IPAC IRSA (every release), one image per pass lined up on the sky as a blink film (GIF) and a difference image, Gaia DR3 stars moved to each date by their proper motions, and a spectrum on the fly from each image's wavelength table, with saturated points marked. The same code comes as one .py file too (pip install astroquery matplotlib reproject).

03How it works

Five small programs (we call them agents; they are ordinary code, not AI models) run in order every time you fly to a spot. Each step is shown live in the Agents panel.

  1. Navigator points the sky at your spot (a name or RA/Dec coordinates).
  2. Archivist asks IRSA for every SPHEREx image covering it, groups them into sky passes (epochs: a gap over 60 days starts a new one) and picks images of the same colour in every pass, so stars look alike.
  3. Aligner downloads small cutouts (8 per pass by default), removes the zodiacal-light background, masks pixels the SPHEREx pipeline flags as bad, reprojects every image onto one common sky grid (the files' own TAN + SIP coordinates) and registers each one on the stars of the first pass (median offset of matched bright stars).
  4. Analyst combines each pass into a median stack (so a cosmic ray or a satellite glint in one image disappears), compares the stacks source by source, and looks inside each pass for anything that sits in one visit and has gone hours later. It asks SkyBoT (or, if that is unreachable, the Minor Planet Center checker) which known objects were there at each image's exact time.
  5. Reporter writes it up: every change gets a status and the evidence behind it, and the report can be downloaded.

All image processing runs in your browser (in background threads, so the page stays responsive). The server only searches the archives, fetches and caches files, and never changes a pixel.

04When does it say "known asteroid"?

Only when every check passes:

  • Position: SPHEREx saw something within 12″ (two SPHEREx pixels) of where SkyBoT puts a known object at that image's time, plus SkyBoT's own position error.
  • Brightness: the object is bright enough for one SPHEREx image (visual magnitude 19 or brighter). Fainter known objects nearby are not credited.
  • Real light, not a glitch: it is also there in the exposure taken minutes earlier or later, or it is as blurred as the stars (a hot pixel or cosmic ray is sharper).
  • Gone at other visits, and its measured motion matches the predicted motion.
Try it: the example "Catch a real asteroid" shows asteroid 3865 Lindbloom passing all checks in real SPHEREx images (RA 189.957°, Dec −4.182°). The example "See a false alarm caught" shows a very strong change that is correctly labelled a hot pixel, not an object.

05How we tested it

Before trusting the method we ran an injection-recovery test, the standard way astronomers measure a search: hide fake planets of known brightness and distance in the real images, with the blur of each field's own stars and the position a distant body would have at each image's time, then let the same agents look for them. We picked 18 sky fields on a fixed grid before the run (ecliptic longitude 0°, 60° … 300° × latitude 0°, +40°, −40°) and publish every field, including where it fails.

06Limits, honestly

  • Only a few passes so far. SPHEREx has made a small number of full-sky passes; some spots have only one, and then nothing can be compared yet.
  • Coarse pixels. One SPHEREx pixel is about 6″ of sky, so small motions within one visit are hard to measure and faint objects blend with noise.
  • Shallow single images. One SPHEREx image is much shallower than the full survey, so ARCNAVE only credits known asteroids of visual magnitude 19 or brighter. Many known asteroids in a field are fainter; ARCNAVE lists them as too faint instead of pretending to see them.
  • Different colours between passes. Each pass may have images at slightly different wavelengths at a given spot, so stars do not cancel perfectly in the Difference view.
  • Crowded Milky Way fields are the hardest: changes and fake planets often land on stars.
  • A Planet Nine as predicted is too faint for SPHEREx. Published estimates put it at about visual magnitude 19 to 24 (Batygin et al. 2019); ARCNAVE catches a slow mover down to about AB 17 in one field. The Hide Planet X test measures how deep the method reaches, not that SPHEREx can see Planet Nine. What it can realistically catch: nearby cold brown dwarfs (WISE 0855, AB 17.15), fast nearby stars, asteroids, and distant bodies much bigger or closer than the predicted Planet Nine.
  • Planets about 300 AU away were missed in some test fields, because the images of one pass are spread over weeks and such a planet moves many pixels between them.
  • New spots take a minute or two the first time. The archive search and about 7 MB per image have to come over the internet (about 24 images for a full run). The example fields and landmarks are stored with the app and open in seconds, and every spot opened once stays fast.
  • Positions of nearby objects (asteroids passing very close to Earth) can be off by about an arcminute, because predictions assume a viewer at Earth's centre.
  • Unconfirmed means unconfirmed. A change that is not a known object could be a variable star, an artefact or something real. Confirming anything new needs follow-up by astronomers.

08Every resource the challenge lists, and how ARCNAVE uses it

  • NASA/IPAC Infrared Science Archive (SPHEREx): every image, found with IRSA's TAP service (spherex.plane, spherex.artifact) and read from NASA's open copy on AWS S3. New releases appear by themselves: SPHEREx Data Release 1 (DR1, November 2026) was picked up with no code change. ARCNAVE's test fields keep the QR2 + QR3 lists their quoted numbers were measured on (the Releases panel says so and can switch a spot to DR1). The SPHEREx Data Explorer, Spectrophotometry Tool and MOST are one click away ("This spot elsewhere").
  • SPHEREx tutorial notebooks (IRSA): the cutout form ?center=ra,dec&size=deg and the TAP query come from them; "Python notebook" gives the same field as a Jupyter notebook in their style. The pipeline's named FLAGS bits (glitches, saturation, ghosts, halos, persistence, snowballs) are used to leave bad pixels out.
  • WiseView: the model for the blink, the Gaia overlay and the WISE overlay. Every star card and the "This spot elsewhere" panel open the same spot in WiseView, so NASA WISE's 2010-2024 images can be blinked next to SPHEREx's.
  • Gaia DR3: every image is aligned on Gaia; motions are checked against it. Asked from IRSA first, then CDS VizieR, then STScI MAST (the copy the challenge names).
  • Canadian Space Agency (NEOSSat, CADC): "This spot elsewhere" asks the Canadian Astronomy Data Centre how many NEOSSat frames cover the spot (Barnard's Star's field: about 650, from November 2018 on) and links to them, to SSOIS (which telescopes imaged an asteroid) and to the Digitized Sky Survey. NEOSSat is pointed at chosen targets in visible light, so most spots have none.
  • Backyard Worlds: a candidate's card runs the volunteers' checks where ARCNAVE can (seen again minutes later, no known asteroid, no Gaia star or CatWISE source within one SPHEREx pixel) and links the rest (SIMBAD within 1′, VizieR motions, IRSA Finder Chart, WiseView), then helps write the report. Nothing is ever called a discovery.
