How ARCNAVE works

Three steps from a place in the sky to an honest answer

The same trick Clyde Tombaugh used to find Pluto in 1930: flip between pictures of one spot. Stars stay put. Anything that jumps is moving.

  1. 01
    190 -4

    Enter coordinates

    A name like Barnard's Star or Andromeda, or a sky position (RA and Dec in degrees).

  2. 02

    Watch the passes play

    ARCNAVE fetches every SPHEREx image of that spot, lines them up on the stars and plays the passes, six months apart, as a film.

  3. 03

    Spot movers and changes, with honest checks

    Blink, subtract, stack and search. Every find is checked against Gaia, WISE and the asteroid catalogues and labelled known, candidate or predicted. Never a discovery.

How we tested it · Limits, honestly

  • No discoveries. An unexplained change is a candidate for an astronomer to check, never a discovery.
  • Planet X is hypothetical. "Hide Planet X" hides a fake planet to measure what SPHEREx could catch. It is not a search.
  • Unknown movers are not linked. An asteroid seen in one pass is far away by the next, and ARCNAVE does not connect the two.
  • Most stars move too little to see: a typical star drifts 0.1 to 0.3″ a year, a few hundredths of a SPHEREx pixel (6.2″). Where nothing moves enough, the film says so and shows the catalogue's prediction, dashed.
  • Spectra are quick aperture photometry, read in about a minute, not a full calibrated spectrum. Very bright stars (Barnard's Star is one) saturate, so their spectrum cannot be measured.

    What is SPHEREx?

    A NASA space telescope launched in March 2025. It maps the whole sky about every six months in 102 colors of near-infrared light, from 0.75 to 5 microns.

    What am I looking at?

    The faint background is an all-sky survey (2MASS by default, or AllWISE or DSS2) shown through Aladin Lite. The colored patch on top is a real SPHEREx spectral image cutout from the IRSA archive. Each SPHEREx image is one narrow slice of wavelength, so the color is false-color.

    Orange and cyan rings are known asteroids, comets and planets, placed by IMCCE SkyBoT (using Minor Planet Center orbits), or by the Minor Planet Center's own checker when SkyBoT cannot be reached, for the exact time each image was taken. A ring does not prove the object is visible: faint ones vanish in a single exposure.

    The agents

    Five small programs run in order when you use Fly to: Navigator points the sky, Archivist finds SPHEREx images and a colour every epoch shares, Aligner downloads cutouts and registers them on the stars, Analyst compares epochs and checks known objects, Reporter writes it up. They are ordinary code, not AI models, and every step shows in the Agents panel.

    Difference view

    With 8 images per epoch, each epoch is first combined into a median stack of its 8 images (same colours in every epoch), so a cosmic ray or a satellite glint in one image disappears before the comparison.

    Subtracts the first epoch from a later one after aligning both on the sky. Stars cancel. A mover leaves an orange blob (where it is now) next to a cyan blob (where it was). Leftover speckle is noise, and images at slightly different wavelengths do not cancel perfectly.

    Planet X

    A hypothesized planet far beyond Neptune, inferred from how some distant icy bodies orbit. It is not confirmed. It would creep across the sky very slowly, so we compare images far apart in time. ARCNAVE does not search for it.

    Hide Planet X

    A test, not a search. You hide a fake planet in the real SPHEREx images of this field: it gets the blur of the field's own stars, your brightness, and the position a body at your distance would have at the exact time of each image (Earth's yearly orbit makes it jump between passes). Then the agents' detector looks for it in two ways: the plain stacks that the agents use on real data, and a motion-following stack (each image shifted back along the planet's path before stacking) for planets that move too far within one pass. The motion-following stack is only used in this test so far. Astronomers call this an injection-recovery test: it measures how faint and how far a planet could be and still be caught. Planet X itself is hypothetical and is not searched for.

    How far can you trust it?

    We tested ARCNAVE on 18 sky fields chosen before the run: how faint a moving planet it can still find, and how often it reports a change when nothing changed. Every field is shown, misses included.

    Glossary

    Epoch
    One full-sky pass, about six months. Grouped here from image dates.
    Visit
    SPHEREx sees a spot several times in each epoch, hours to days apart. An asteroid moves between visits; a star does not.
    Blink comparator
    Flip between two images of one field. Stars stay. Movers jump.
    Proper motion
    A star's slow drift across the sky over years.
    Parallax
    A small yearly wobble caused by Earth's orbit. Closer objects wobble more.
    False-color
    Invisible light mapped to colors you can see.

    How far can you trust it?

    Four tests on the same 18 sky fields, picked on a grid before the runs (along the ecliptic every 60°, on it and 40° above and below). Nothing was left out: fields where ARCNAVE does badly are in the tables too.

    1. Can it find a faint, slow planet?

    We hid fake planets in the real images and let the agents' detector look for them, with the plain stacks and with a motion-following stack.

    Loading the validation scorecard...

    2. How often does it cry wolf?

    From two passes of a field we build two stacks that hold the same colours and the same mix of dates, so nothing real differs between them. Every "change" the agents still report there is a false alarm. As a check, 16 fake stars are added to one stack, and the agents must find them.

    Loading the false-alarm test...

    3. Does it notice a star getting brighter?

    We made real stars brighter by a known amount in every image of the last pass and checked whether the Changes view reports them. The rule is that a star must gain at least a quarter of its light, so +10 % should not be reported and +50 % should.

    Loading the brightness test...

    4. Can it find a star that moves?

    We added fake moving stars of known speed and brightness to the real images of each field (in every image, at the place their motion had taken them by that date), measured them, and let the deep search look for them without knowing where they were. The images were put back exactly afterwards.

    What the labels mean

    Confirmed known asteroid
    Position, brightness and shape all match an asteroid the Minor Planet Center already knows. The only label you can rely on.
    Likely artefact
    Sharper than any star, or missing from most images: a cosmic-ray hit, hot pixel or glint.
    Unconfirmed
    Nothing explains it. A candidate to check, never a discovery. In the Milky Way most of these are noise.
    Possible variable star
    A star that gained at least a quarter of its light in some image (test 3).

    What it can and cannot see

    Can
    Spot what appears, vanishes or moves between SPHEREx passes and between the visits of one pass; name known asteroids and comets from the catalogue and check them (position, brightness, shape); tell you how faint a slow planet could be and still be caught on the field you are looking at (Hide Planet X).
    Cannot yet
    Brown dwarfs: most drift under an arcsecond a year and even the fastest known only a few, less than one SPHEREx pixel (6.2″) between passes six months apart, so their motion does not show. Unknown movers: an asteroid seen in one pass is far away in the next, and ARCNAVE does not link the two. Searching real data with the motion-following stack: it is used in the Hide Planet X test only; the agents do not yet run it on real fields. Crowded Milky Way fields: most changes there are noise (test 2).
    Not tested
    How often fast asteroids and comets are caught; stars that get fainter.
    Never claimed
    No discovery. Planet X is a hypothesis and is not searched for. An unconfirmed change is a candidate for an astronomer to check.
